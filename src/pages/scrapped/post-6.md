As I have grown and gone through life, how I have seen others' perspectives on the tasks they do daily, whether it be for school, their job, their home, or their hobbies, has always fascinated me. How one task can be so exciting and easy and another can be dreadful and frankly frustrating; but more than that, which of these tasks we choose to base our subsistence off of, and which ones we don't.

Growing up, I've always been told that if I'm lucky, I can make a lot of money doing something I really love—in other words, that ideally, I could make a job out of my passion. In contradiction, I've also been surrounded by messages in the media and personal experiences that if I make my passion my job, I will probably fall out of love with it. These two ideas exist in contrast with one another and it leads me to question the very paradigm of a "job" and what it means for our livelihoods.

In our world, a job is not just a cycle of tasks, nor is it simply a role, it's an *identity*—a teacher does not just teach, they *are a teacher*, an engineer does not simply go to work and engineer things, they in many ways are an engineer in their identity. When we ask kids about what job they'll have when they're older, we don't ask them what they want to do, or what kind of job they actually want, we ask them what they want to *be*, or rather, *who* they want to be.

This alone may not seem to be much of a shocker. These jobs are roles that we fit into, and by fitting into them, they shape who we feel ourselves to be. But this highlights an important point that labor is not merely something to offer up into the world, not something to trade for subsistence or survival, nor is it something we passively and neutrally participate in for some extrinsic purpose. It is something that gets to the heart of ourselves. It is something that is necessarily meaningful to your identity and your personhood.

## Childhood Labor

As children, we all commonly labor every day. From the moment we wake up and brush our teeth, get ourselves dressed and pour ourselves a bowl of cereal, we are laboring with a specific purpose: to take care of our body. To me, this seems to be the most essential form of labor: self-care. Sometimes it doesn't feel like labor, other times it most certainly does. But either way, this is a form of labor that we actively choose. We may not enjoy it. But we do it for ourselves.

When I would get home from school as a kid, I would often turn to my game console, put in the Minecraft disc, and start playing the game. When I played Minecraft, in many ways, I was quite literally laboring. I would have tasks to plan out, goals to achieve, projects to complete, and would spend hours *working* to do so. Inviting my sister, we'd work together to clear some space in a forest and build a house. We'd dive deep into the earth in the search for diamonds. We'd spend some time building a mob grinder for XP. To me, each and every bit of this was laborious. Yet we both enjoyed it, we both chose it, we even called it *play*.

This gets into the heart of childhood labor: for children, labor is not exclusively a dreadful thing. It is not simply something to mourn and regret and hate. It is something often chosen purposefully and done happily. I loved playing Minecraft. It wasn't merely labor, nor was it simply play—it was art.

If I have a job where I am assigned to write an article a month, I will probably cry a few times while grudging through the job. But if I have the opportunity to write a blogpost in my own free time, I will excitedly look forward to planning out and brainstorming a plan for my next writing, and I will be happy when working on it. (Of course, it is not quite that simple. Even our passion projects can become frustrating at times. The point is, however, that the lack of coercion allows me to treat as a game.)

This distinction between "work" and "play" fascinates me. Why are some tasks considered "labor" and others considered "play"? When thinking about the structure of our society, I have noticed some people will become "stuck" in the way things currently are. I recall one person explaining that the reason why college can't be free is that we need some people to do the jobs nobody else wants to do. This implies that our society requires that some people be coerced into punishing labor by virtue of reality. And in our current society, this is certainly true to an extent. But what I am most curious about is why we define certain tasks as the following:

1. Necessary—these tasks cannot be creatively phased out;
2. Universally sufferable—none can take pleasure in them (and therefore there is no point in experimenting in making them less dreadful); and
3. Individual—one person must do them, they cannot be a shared burden, nor can they inspire community.

Why, instead of questioning why some people must suffer so others can prosper, do we simply accept the way things are in this moment and deny ourselves the creative agency to choose otherwise? To engineer a clever solution, to make a grueling task fun? Do we not have an obligation not only to ourselves but to the people we tell they must do the work nobody else wants to do to reject that there is no alternative?

I say we do. So why do we not fight to solve the jobs that make life so miserable, make them fun or at least make it so we can drudge through them together and make them just a bit less miserable? It seems rather heinous to sit with our hands under our thighs and say, "Sorry, nothing I can do about it." Should we not stand up and find ways to diminish the workload of these tasks, make them social, and, dare I say it, make them *playful*?

I say we must. Returning to the heart of the conversation, what I refer to as "childhood labor" is not simply the labor that a child does—frankly, I do not believe that much of the labor children do on a daily basis *is* childhood labor, between schooling and how we often position chores. Rather, childhood labor is the act of treating labor unseriously. It is an act of labor faced with a playful glint in one's eyes. It is the labor where one invites one's loved ones over to them to partake in their labor. It's when I get mischievously in the morning and think, "Holy moly, the kitchen is a mess—*imagine the looks on their faces when they see I did it all. >:)*" It's when I sit down to write something and treat it not as a task to complete but as an opportunity to think.

I think we can all learn something from the kids who create with passion, because it is not that they are somehow exceptional and that it is the natural way of things for us to lose that, but rather it is that they have not yet been taught to toss aside their enjoyment of doing what they choose to do, not yet been taught that it is our labor that we are expected to extract every ounce from our bodies to survive in this fucked up world.

## What Is Art?

In the first class of a course on Native American Art and Culture, my Native American professor asked us to give words that, to us, were representative of art. Many people gave out excellent answers like "creative," "challenging," "expressive," even "critical" or "political"—all reasonable answers, yes? The professor thought so. And then she explained that in Native American languages, there isn't really a word for "art." Not even a concept. "Art" doesn't really exist. But that can't be right, can it? I mean, if any of you have been to a museum that showcased Native American culture, I'm sure you've seen plenty of Native American art. So how could it be that art doesn't exist?

She went on to explain that in Native American cultures, everything is symbolic and a part of their belief systems. When they made a pot, they painted and decorated it with their expressive and spiritual imagery, but not to make it "artistic"—rather, to make it *be*.

In western cultures, we tend to view art as an expressive *discipline*. Art is something you learn, a skill you hone, a tool you use to produce something beautiful or evocative. It's something purposeful and separate from other doings. When you make art, you're not really doing much other than the art. Whether you're painting, writing, sculpting, whatever, it can in some way be reduced down to doing art.

Interestingly, even in schools, art is considered its own separate thing. When I was in High School, one of the credits I needed to graduate was in "Performing Arts"—I satisfied this through performing music. Another friend satisfied their art requirement by taking an "art" class. In college, for a general education requirement, I needed to take an art class, so I took one on Film. I learned about the "art of film" by watching, analyzing, and writing about films.

As a social science major, most of my classes (if not all) have a major element of writing to them. Last semester, even taking a relatively light courseload, I wrote a total of 5 papers, including one rather lengthy one that ended up being almost 20-pages. But none of this counted towards my art credit. Why would it? I wasn't really doing any art was I?

Well, why wasn't I? In western cultures, we designate art to be its own thing, it gets its own namespace, has its own requirements and is itself a requirement. You have to "do art" to get a diploma. So who defines what art is? What if I want to define it? I'm going to define it.

### My Art

When I wrote my final topic paper last semester (that really big one), I felt I was making art. Not in the grandiose "this is a modern classic" kind of way (obviously), but rather, in the "I'm proud of this, I'm proud of my words, and I'm proud of what I've learned," kind of way. This is what kept me going through the writing journey, kept me excited every step of the way, kept me coming back to it time and time again to rewrite, touch up, add to, reflect on, all the way up till the hour it was due. It was that notion that I wasn't just creating something to tick off the box and score some points, nor was I even writing it just to learn (which itself is a lovely goal). It was like I was creating something that, deep down, I needed to create. Like I didn't really have any other choice but to write it for my own sake.

Why do we gatekeep and restrict ourselves from the idea that we are all creating art everyday? Why is it that when I make food in the morning I am simply making food and yet when a chef makes some fancy dish they suddenly are allowed to call it art? It seems to me that there is a degree of gatekeeping by the order of "quality" when it comes to art. We might say some slop called *Henry Puffer* is art, but for some reason, the words I write into this website aren't.

What if we didn't do that? What if, instead of art being something specific and precise, a standard to be held to, art was simply a matter of doing? When I make coffee with my Moka Pot, I feel as though there's a part of me going into that drink, and it feels expressive in its own light. It feels cathartic and euphoric to reframe your everyday tasks as an expression of yourself rather than a mere chore to get through. To reframe writing for my classes as artistic expression rather than simply throwing down my ideas onto paper.

## What Labor Could Be

At the beginning of this blogpost, I argued that labor is often seen as a grueling, miserable, depressing task that nobody really wants to do. But it can be more. It can be fun, social, cathartic, expressive, artistic, enjoyable, it can be something you look forward to every morning, it can be something to take pride and pleasure in, it can be something that, with a little creativity, we move from the crux of Sisyphus's struggle to something we are lucky to experience.

Am I saying that no labor can suck? No, of course not. We probably will always have to do things we don't want to do, at least in the moment. But this is not what life should be defined by. To reduce life's labor to a miserable crawl from one end to the other is to reduce life itself to nothing more than suffering.

I would like to see a world where we instead make labor to be playful, fun, social, and/or artistic. Where our learning is chosen and taken pleasure in, we clean with loved ones and chatting or singing, we cook together and serve one another and live off of creation.

The first step of creating that world is rejecting that this is simply the way it is, and there is no changing that.
