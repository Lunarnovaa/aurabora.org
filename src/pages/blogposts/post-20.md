I don't need to quote it, we all know what I'm referencing. But of these three things which the Declaration of Independence fights for, this one really is the odd one out. Life, something that one is born with, the very medium of existence; liberty, something I might argue is not "natural" per se but a kind of quality/characteristic of any given way of culture, something continuously negotiated and recreated;[^1] and the pursuit of happiness. Not a thing, not a collective quality, not something any of us are born with. Just a direction. Happiness itself is not something that we, socially, should strive to create or cultivate; just something that we, individually, should pursue.

[^1]: Just to be clear, I don't think the "founding fathers" would disagree with me here―famously, they believed liberty was something the state was necessary to protect. I disagree that we need coercion to be free (rather paradoxical, to me), but not that we need sociality to be free. Mostly, our disagreement seems to be that I do not believe sociality requires violence.

I think this is interesting because it implies happiness is a rather individual problem. I also think this is interesting because it positions happiness as a kind of complex "forever-project" of life. Life is your medium; liberty, your muse; happiness, your art. Happiness becomes the measure of success, and something *one works hard* to create. The Protestant work ethic manifest, in a way. Work hard, and you will find the happiness God has promised to hard workers.

But what actually is happiness?

I've struggled a lot with this term. Happiness really is the thing that I've considered most important, and I've felt a bit of cognitive dissonance with that. Because I haven't actually really been happy. Not like I think I should. Over the past five years, what I term my conscious life, I've been very depressed much more than not depressed. But during much of this time, the times I've felt "happy," have been rather Hedonistic. I have quite a distaste for Hedonism. So imagine my discomfort when it feels like the only happiness that is not alien to me is a Hedonistic one. And I realize only now, looking in my rear-view mirror at that past of mine, that happiness is not "the" goal, it's a survival mechanism.

In my experience, there are two ways to survive trauma: blocking it out in the moment, and blocking it out in the past. The latter is the displacement of memory. The former is the use of a myriad of techniques: dissociation, escapism; engulfing oneself in novels, in stories, in film, in television; but also indulging in pleasure: video games, drugs, limerence, sex. I think it is pleasure that is the form of happiness I am most familiar with.

So pleasure is a survival mechanism. It allows you to survive. But I think the pursuit of happiness actually goes beyond this. In my piece on [sociopolitical creativity](/blogposts/post-17), I mentioned that my imagination of what my future could look like, a future where I am surrounded by love and freedom and happiness, gave me the will to continue on. It made me believe that I was in the pursuit of happiness. And while pleasure allowed me to survive trauma on a day to day, it was the belief in this pursuit that allowed me to continue on year to year. 

I worry that this isn't enough anymore. I find it harder and harder to believe in a future that hasn't fully manifest itself. But I think in the moment, we can still cultivate small pieces of the future in small pockets of our lives―I feel this is the only way to truly survive beyond the moment to moment.

Happiness is ephemeral. It is momentary by nature. There are times when I am so sad I know the only thing to do is to write poetry, and there are times when I am so sad I feel the only thing to do is to be with my loved ones. And these generate happiness; I feel happy in that moment. But then, I leave this space of generation, and the happiness leaves me, and I am again sad. This is why I no longer believe in the pursuit of happiness. Happiness is not something you pursue. It is not something you can have. It is not something you can possess. Happiness is not property, like the Lockean origins of that phrase suggests; happiness is not an estate. Happiness, like liberty, is only cultivated.

What then, generates happiness? I don't think this is something that can be enumerated. But something I have found, is that to sit within this garden of happiness, whether it bears fruit in the moment or not, itself grants a healing sensation.

Because just like how the only thing that can allow someone to really, actually, escape trauma is a change of environment, so too is the only thing that can allow someone to heal from trauma a particular kind of environment: I feel this environment is one of abundance.

Traumatizing environments are ones where we run, and run. We run street to street, house to house, putting everything we can into this sprint. We run from pleasure to pleasure, or try to dissociate from our bodies, try not to feel our bodymind aching in the sprint, try not to feel. It is only these brief vestiges of pleasure or dissociation that give us the drive to continue on. It is a life of scarcity. A life where every day, we work so that we may store up enough pleasure to eat through the week. It is a life of scarcity of happiness; we live in the pursuit of it to survive.

I want to argue, then, that a liberatory life is one in which we do not pursue happiness. One in which we don't need to. I feel, I know by feeling, that the cure to trauma, the solution to this life of depression, is to stop having to run, to cultivate and care for and truly love the garden around you that bears the fruit in which you indulge. I feel that it is only when we no longer have to pursue the thing that allows us to survive, when we no longer have to pursue survival, that living stops being so fucking traumatizing.

I feel that it is not by curing ourselves of our sadness, or maximizing our bounty of happiness in our sprint, or being free to run as hard and as fast as we possibly can, as if that is at all a freedom, that should be our ultimate goal. I feel deeply, that survival, happiness, and life, just like liberty, are things we can only find abundance in together. And I know, then, that an individualist happiness is inherently doomed to impermanence.
